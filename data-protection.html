07 · Running it for real

Faces are special-category data. Here is what you would need to measure your own users.

What this project processes, on what basis, what it has deliberately not done, and what an operator would need in place to run the same measurement on the people actually enrolled in their system. A reflection under UK and EU GDPR by the person whose face is the data, not legal advice.

Why this page exists

The measurement this project makes is only meaningful about people when it is made on a real enrolled identity: it asks how far a specific person can be altered before a specific deployed system stops recognising them. Synthetic identities answer questions about the instrument — whether the method works, what the host proxy is good for, how much of a result belongs to the generator — and a pilot of twenty has been measured on exactly that basis, and is reported as a pilot rather than a population. What they cannot do is tell an operator about their own users. That is biometric processing twice over — the enrolment, and then the generation and scoring of altered images of that person’s face. Under Article 9 of the GDPR, biometric data used to identify someone is special-category data: prohibited unless one of a short list of conditions applies, and even then subject to the ordinary principles of purpose, minimisation, security and transparency.

The project’s position is that the honest route to a population measurement is an operator building a dataset of their own users, with explicit consent and adequate controls — not scraped faces, and not synthetic faces standing in for people. Everything below is written to make that route concrete.

The principles, applied

  • Purpose limitation. One purpose: measuring how an authentication system behaves on an identity. The data is not used to train a recogniser, to identify anyone, or to infer anything about a person beyond that behaviour.
  • Minimisation. One anchor per identity; generated images kept only as long as the measurement needs them; published outputs are aggregates and, for the author alone, illustrative renders.
  • Security. Local processing; no third-party inference services; the device under test holds only the enrolled template it would hold in deployment.
  • Transparency. This page, the open methodology, and a status page that records what was withdrawn and why.
  • No re-identification. Synthetic identities are compared with each other only to describe how crowded the space is; no attempt is made to match a synthetic face to a real person.

What an operator would need

An organisation that has deployed a vendor’s face-verification system and wants to know how it behaves on its own enrolled users — rather than on the vendor’s benchmark — is the audience this measurement was built for. Running it on real users means:

  • An Article 9 condition, and explicit consent is the honest default. Consent to enrol is not consent to this. The measurement needs its own, specific, informed and freely given consent: that altered images of the participant’s face will be generated and scored to test the system’s behaviour on their identity, with the results reported in aggregate. Employers and public bodies should be cautious about the imbalance of power that can undermine consent, and may need another Article 9 condition alongside a statutory basis; that is a question for their DPO.
  • A data protection impact assessment (Article 35). Biometric processing of this kind is squarely in the high-risk category; the DPIA should cover the generation of altered faces as a distinct processing activity, and the retention of generated images and embeddings.
  • Controller and processor roles settled in the vendor contract. Who holds the templates, who may run the system off-line for testing, and whether the vendor’s terms permit it.
  • Retention and deletion. Generated images and embeddings deleted once boundaries are extracted; a participant’s withdrawal deletes their anchor, templates and generated data, with the aggregate re-computed without them.
  • On-premises processing and no publication of faces. Results leave the organisation as distributions, not images.
  • No children, and a review by eye rather than trust in an automatic filter.
  • Reporting by group where equality duties require it, with the caveat that self-reported attributes are the only defensible labels.

With those in place, an operator gets what a vendor’s FAR cannot give: for each person actually enrolled, on the device actually installed, the shape of their accepted region, how far it reaches towards their nearest enrolled colleague, and the spread of both across the cohort they are responsible for — the operator cohort protocol.

This project has produced both failure modes. A measurement-path mismatch — enrolment running on a different image buffer from the judges — scored every INT8 result against a template the deployed system would never make, and went undetected until 2026-09-18. And reading an individual’s result off the faster host proxy misorders exactly the people it matters for: across twenty-one identities five device medians fell outside the host’s own interval and the identity the host called narrowest was not the one the chip did.

Why the age control is a human one: the estimator failed, and no threshold would have caught it. On 2026-09-18 this project searched for synthetic identities near the subject’s, and screened the candidates with genderage.onnx — the age model shipped with InsightFace. Of the six nearest frontal proposals, the one ranked fourth was scored age 47. On visual review it is a young child. Not a borderline teenager who might reasonably be read as an adult: a small child returned as middle-aged. It was deleted and quarantined.

The number matters more than the incident. An estimator that is merely noisy can be made safe with a margin — refuse anything under 25, under 30, under 40. This estimate cleared the eligibility bar of 18 by 29 years. To have rejected this face on the estimate alone, the gate would have to refuse everything scored below 47 — which refuses most of the adult population with it. There is no threshold that removes this failure and leaves the method usable. The estimator cannot be a gate, and it cannot be a backstop behind a human check either, because a reviewer who knows a model is also looking is a reviewer looking less carefully.

This generalises past this project. Any pipeline that proposes identities by similarity to a target face will surface minors, because similarity in an embedding space is not conditioned on age — and the more effective the search, the more reliably it will do so. An operator adopting this method inherits that property. The control is review by eye of every materialised identity, before anything is generated towards it, by a named person, recorded. It is slower and it does not scale, which is the honest cost of the method rather than an argument against the control.

An example consent form

A starting point for an operator, in plain language, to be adapted with their DPO. Square brackets are the operator’s to fill.

Taking part in a test of [system name] on your identity

Who is asking. [Organisation], which operates the face-verification system you are enrolled in. Data protection contact: [DPO name, email].

What we want to do. Measure how the system behaves on your face, not on an average. To do that we will take the photographs you are already enrolled with (or [number] new ones), and use a computer model to generate altered versions of your face — changed by controlled amounts in many directions — and score each one with the system to find where it stops recognising you. The result is a set of numbers describing that region. Some of the generated images will, by construction, be images the system accepts as you.

What data. Your enrolment photographs; the mathematical representation of your face the system uses (a template); the generated images and their representations; the scores. Nothing else about you. We will not estimate your age, ethnicity or anything else from your face. If you choose to tell us characteristics for group reporting, you do so in the box below, and only what you write is used.

What we will not do. Use this data to train any model. Share it with the system’s vendor or any third party. Send it to any cloud service. Publish your face or any generated image of it. Use it for any purpose other than this test.

Where and how long. On [organisation]’s own equipment at [site], encrypted. Generated images and representations are deleted within [30 days] of the test; the resulting numbers are kept for [period] in aggregate form. This consent expires on [date]. If we want to repeat the test after that, or on a new version of the system, we will ask you again.

Your rights. You can withdraw at any time, without giving a reason and without any effect on your use of the system, by contacting [contact]. We will delete your photographs, template, generated images and results, recompute any aggregate without you, and confirm in writing. You can ask what we hold about you at any time.

The risks, honestly. A face cannot be changed if it leaks; that is why we keep as little as possible for as short a time as possible, encrypted. The generated images are ones the system accepts as you and are treated as sensitive for that reason. Taking part is voluntary; your employment / access / service does not depend on it.

Where this sits in the rules

Two regimes are usually named when a face-recognition deployment is questioned. Neither asks for what this project measures, and the reasons are worth stating precisely, because the gaps are the argument.

The EU AI Act: a face unlock is probably not the high-risk case

Annex III of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 makes biometric systems high-risk, and the obligations that follow — a risk-management system, data governance including examination for bias, and declared accuracy and robustness metrics — are close to what an assurance programme would want. But the biometric entry is aimed at remote biometric identification (one face against many), and biometric verification whose sole purpose is confirming that someone is who they claim to be is carved out of it.

A phone or door unlock is verification. So the systems that are deployed in the largest numbers, against the most people, are the ones least likely to attract the Annex III duties — and a per-identity measurement of the kind on this site is not required of them by anybody. Where it does bite is when the same model is put to identification, or used in one of the Annex III contexts such as employment or border control; then the accuracy and robustness obligations apply, and the question becomes which accuracy, measured how.

That is where this work has something to say. The metric a vendor declares is a population false-match and false-non-match rate. The finding here is that a population rate conceals per-identity behaviour: on one threshold, across twenty-one identities, the measured acceptance region ranged from 55° to 68°. A declared FAR is true of the population and silent about the person in front of the sensor.

NIST: demographic groups, not individuals

NIST's face-recognition evaluations, and the demographic-effects work in particular, measure differentials between demographic groups — how false-match rates vary by sex, age band and region of origin. That is a real and important axis, and it is not this one.

This project measures spread within any such group: two identities that would land in the same demographic cell differed here by about thirteen degrees of boundary, and the pilot's between-identity spread (sd 2.4–3.6°) sits alongside a within-identity, across-direction spread of 4.2–5.0°. Group parity does not imply individual parity, and a system could pass a demographic audit while still giving two people in the same cell materially different security. The two measurements are complementary; neither substitutes for the other.

This is a reading, not legal advice, by someone whose face is the data rather than a lawyer — the same caveat as the rest of this page. The carve-out for verification, and how a given deployment is classified, are exactly the points to put to someone qualified before relying on them. What is not in doubt is the measurement: a single declared threshold produced materially different acceptance regions for different people, and neither regime currently asks anyone to check that.

If you want to run this

The honest limit of this project is its sample. One real identity — the author's, measured to 1,024 directions on the deployed chip — and twenty synthetic ones. That is enough to show the method works, to show that per-identity variation is real and large, and to show that a host stand-in cannot be trusted for an individual. It is not enough to tell an operator what the spread looks like across their enrolled users, which is the question that matters.

That study needs a consented cohort of real people enrolled in a real deployment, and it needs to be run by whoever operates such a system, under their own ethics approval. It is not something this project can do for itself, and it is not waiting on anything here.

Everything required to run it is public: the method is in the experiment chain, the procedure is in the repository's playbook, the controls and an example consent form are above, and the code is on GitHub. If a group wants to take this on and will do the ethics properly, the author is glad to support it. The sample-size arithmetic is already measured rather than guessed: 128 directions give an identity's median boundary to about half a degree, and resolving a real three-degree difference between two people needs roughly thirty directions each.

What remains open

Three questions this project has not resolved. Whether a generator trained on photographs collected without biometric consent can be an acceptable instrument for a consented study, even when it never reproduces those photographs. Whether a synthetic face that happens to resemble a real person is that person’s data. And the dual-use fact that an instrument which moves a face towards someone by a known amount is also a tool for impersonation, which argues for access control and no public release of any model built for it. The status page carries these under future work.
