02 · The argument

Two enrolled people, same system, same threshold — not necessarily the same protection

Companion to Why. That note argues why aggregate metrics leave an institution with nothing to challenge. This one is about the specific thing they hide: that two enrolled people do not get the same protection from the same system, and that nothing in the standard evidence pack would tell you.

Where the unevenness comes from

Recognition models map faces onto a unit hypersphere — for a 512-dimensional embedding, S511 — and are trained with margin-based losses that optimise separability over a training distribution. Two things follow that are well documented in the literature and not controversial:

  • training corpora are unevenly distributed across demographic and presentation attributes; and
  • identity is not cleanly disentangled from those attributes, so demographic and soft-biometric traits (age, apparent gender, skin tone, hairstyle, accessories, facial structure) occupy some of the same embedding capacity as identity itself.

The consequence for an individual is geometric. If attributes are entangled with identity, some templates land in a spacious, well-separated region of the sphere and others land in a crowded one. Two enrolled people, same system, same threshold — different amounts of room before someone else is close enough.

First measured 2026-09-18 · host and device rows

Identities differ by about as much as directions do. Twenty synthetic identities measured on the same 128 directions as the subject, on the deployed INT8 pipeline: the median boundary at the vendor threshold ranges from 55° to 68° across identities (between-identity spread 2.4–3.6° by source; within any one identity, across directions, 4.2–5.0°). The subject, at 67.4°, sits at the top of that range. “The deployed model is looser than the desktop one” — true of the subject by 6.7° — is not true in general: per identity the INT8 boundary sits anywhere from 7° inside the desktop model’s to 6° outside it. What is general is unevenness: the INT8 pipeline’s boundary is the most direction-dependent for every identity (spread across directions 4.2–5.0° against the desktop model’s 2.4°). And exposure is unequal in the way that matters to an operator: at the vendor threshold, for the most crowded identities 87–98% of directions reach beyond where their nearest other identity sits; for the most isolated, 0–3%; the subject, 1%. That exposure is driven by how near the neighbour is (correlation −0.8), not by the region being wider for crowded people (−0.1 to −0.4). Every identity was then re-judged on the chip itself and every finding above survived: surfaces reproduce within about a degree, and the cohort spread is the same. What does not carry over is the individual — per direction host and device differ by about 2°, and the host’s narrowest identity measures 3.5° wider on silicon, so the three narrowest come out in a different order. Synthetic identities, one render per point; the same measurement on real, consenting users is the intended end use.

Why population metrics cannot answer it

Fairness evaluation is normally cohort-level: compare FMR/FNMR, or TAR at a fixed FAR, across demographic groups. That work establishes that differentials exist. It cannot tell an individual, or the risk owner responsible for them, anything about a specific enrolled template, for two reasons:

  • Group averages mask individuals. Demographic parity or equalised odds describe how a cohort fares on average. Within any cohort there will be templates with generous, stable boundaries and templates sitting against a cliff. The average says nothing about which one you enrolled.
  • Rates have no direction. A false-match rate counts how often the system is wrong. It never says along which axis of change it becomes wrong — whether this face is robust to ageing but fragile to a lighting shift, or the reverse. Two systems with identical FAR can fail on entirely different changes.

What IAS can ask instead

The question changes from “is the dataset biased against a group?” to “what is the geometric tolerance of this specific enrolled identity, on this specific deployed system?”

Diagnostic questionHow it is measuredWhat the answer tells a risk owner
Does this face have weaker discriminatory power than a peer’s?Concentric shells of tangent perturbations around each anchor, parameterised by target cosine; boundary bisected per directionCompares angular tolerance (θ = arctan ε) and the shape of the accepted region between enrolled people
Is this template near a decision cliff?Score trajectory recorded along every ray, with per-ray fragility and plateau flagsDistinguishes a score that falls away smoothly from one that plunges at a small perturbation
Does low-power hardware affect this face disproportionately?The same probes scored by the quantised model (SiL and HiL) and by float models of the same and larger sizeSeparates what quantisation costs from what the small parameter budget costs — per identity, not per benchmark

The geometry is exact: perturbations are drawn in the tangent space orthogonal to the anchor, so a perturbation of size ε sits at angle θ from the anchor.

θ = arctan ε,   cos θ = 1 / √(1 + ε²)

Shells are specified by the cosine a discriminator actually thresholds on, and ε and θ follow from it. See why SiL and HiL for what the SiL and HiL judges in that table actually are and what each comparison isolates.

What is honest to claim today

  • The perturbation geometry is exact and the generator is deterministic; but a step in the generator’s latent is not a pure identity step — pose, hair and background move with the face — so every perturbation’s size is measured after rendering (to within 1°) rather than set, in one recogniser’s space. That recogniser is the ruler; the desktop reference judge is nearly the ruler measuring itself.
  • Scale: one identity on 1,024 directions, all five judges, host and device; twenty synthetic identities on 128 directions each, host and device. One render per point.
  • Synthetic identities, from two generators that pack faces differently, with prompt-derived or model-estimated labels used only to spread the sample. Cross-identity claims about real people wait for a consented real cohort.
  • Exposure is radius against radius: a boundary compared with the distance to a neighbour lying in another direction. The path from one identity towards its neighbour — the actual impersonation measurement — is next.
  • The device is the arbiter and the host is a valid proxy: over 1,024 rays the ESP32-S3 reproduces the host to +0.4 ± 2.0° per direction.
  • A correction of 2026-09-18: enrolment had run on the full-size image while the judges use the pipeline’s 320-px buffer; re-judged on the right buffer the subject’s boundary moved 0.2°. Recorded, not hidden.

Stating this precisely matters more than usual here. The purpose of the tool is to let someone challenge a claim with evidence; a tool that overstates its own evidence cannot do that.
