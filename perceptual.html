Interactive perceptual test

Can you tell her from a stranger?

The recognition model in this project says a render of the subject scores higher against her enrolled template than her own photographs score against each other. Below are sixteen pairs it was asked to judge. See if you can do better than it did — or than she could.

Nothing is recorded

This page collects nothing. Every answer is held in a variable in your browser tab and compared to the key by JavaScript running locally; nothing is sent anywhere, logged, or stored. Reload the page and it is gone. There is no server behind this test.

All sixteen images are synthetic: generated from a face-identity embedding by a generative renderer, not photographs of any real person. Half of each pair is a render conditioned on the subject’s own anchor embedding — the identity her template was built from. The other half is a render of the synthetic identity the recognition model finds most confusable with her, out of 1,164 candidates. You are not told which is which, and there is no reference photo on this page to compare them against — because there wasn’t one for the model either. It made its call from the embedding alone.

What the model says versus what a person sees

In the project’s own recognition space, over the subject’s nine real photographs and renders of her anchor:

ComparisonCosine similarity to her enrolled template
Renders of her own anchor embedding0.816 median (0.751–0.840)
Her nine real photographs, pairwise0.754 median (0.624–0.836)
1,164 synthetic impostors0.269 max

A render built purely from her identity embedding sits closer to her template, by the model’s own metric, than her actual photographs sit to each other.

And yet, shown these renders, the subject does not recognise herself in them. That is the gap this test asks you to feel rather than take on trust: a face can sit comfortably inside the “genuine” region of a recognition system while not reading as that person to a human at all — not to a stranger, and not always to the person herself. See Discrimination for why population metrics can’t catch this, and the wider threshold picture behind the 0.269 figure above.

How to read your score

Chance on sixteen binary trials is 8/16, and with only sixteen trials the ordinary spread around chance is wide — anywhere from about 5 to 11 is unremarkable even for pure guessing, so treat one run as suggestive, not conclusive.

  • Near 8/16: the renders are not giving you, personally, any usable identity signal. That is consistent with what this project measured: a model-genuine score with no human-perceptible correlate.
  • Well above 8/16: either you got lucky, or these particular renders do carry some cue a human can use — expression, framing, something about the impostor pool’s style — worth being sceptical of rather than assuming it generalises.

The comparison that actually matters here is not your score against chance. It is your score against hers. The subject is the only observer whose judgement is authoritative about her own face, because she is the only person who genuinely knows what she looks like. A stranger scoring at chance is consistent with the renders lacking human-legible identity; it doesn’t prove it the way her own non-recognition does. Strangers guessing at random is compatible with several stories — some renders might be recognisable to people who know her, for instance, if you happen to. Her not recognising herself rules that out directly.
