What a ray is

An anchor is one real identity: the centroid embedding of nine of Dr James’ photos, a single point in the recognition model’s 512-dimensional space. A ray is one fixed direction out from that point. Twenty directions were drawn once, early in the pilot, and kept fixed; walking out along a ray means rendering a face at increasing angular distance from the anchor and asking the edge model whether it still recognises it as the enrolled identity.

What the boundary means

For each ray, shells-refine bisects to find where the discriminator’s verdict flips from accept to reject, to a tolerance of ±0.03 in the perturbation radius ε. Each test point is the median of three renders (majority verdict), so one unlucky render — a hand across the face, odd lighting — can’t set the boundary on its own. The result is an angle: how far, in degrees, the identity can drift along that direction before the ESP-DL model (running at its default acceptance threshold, tau = 0.5) stops accepting it.

Why a flat circle would be the ideal

If a discriminator treated every direction of drift identically, its boundary would be a single radius repeated at every angle — a perfect circle. That’s the dashed line in the polar plot: not a theoretical target, just the mean of the 18 bounded rays (32.7°), drawn out as a circle so the real shape can be compared against a direction-blind one. The measured boundary is nothing like it. It’s a star: some directions hold the identity firmly, others give way after a handful of degrees.

What the 7.2× means for assurance

7.2× is the ratio between the most forgiving bounded direction (ray 18, 49.5°) and the least (rays 8 and 14, tied at 6.8°). For this identity and this system, how far a face can drift before recognition stops accepting it depends on which way it drifts by a factor of seven. A single number — “accepts up to X degrees of change” — would be wrong by that factor depending on direction, and an assurance case that only probes the forgiving directions could look solid while the narrow ones are already unsafe. Which directions those are, and whether they trace something meaningful (e.g. an INT8 quantisation artefact versus a property of face recognition generally), is the open question the next pass and a float-precision comparison are for.

Read this pilot for what it is

  • One identity, 20 directions. Not a population result — a first measurement that the shape holds at all.
  • Renders come from Arc2Face, so its presentation noise (hair, lighting, styling redrawn per seed) is inside the measurement, not filtered out.
  • The edge model’s first-stage face detector was relaxed to a score threshold of 0.3 (vendor default 0.5) because raw renders sit under the default — see Status for the compositing trial that trades this off against plate-identity bias.
  • This is the software-in-the-loop runner, not the physical ESP32-S3 board. Host-vs-silicon numerical parity is still unverified — see SiL & HiL.
  • 2 of the 20 rays never flipped inside the sampled range and are right-censored at the dataset’s edge (cos 0.60, ≈53°); their true boundary is somewhere further out, unmeasured.
  • A second, higher-resolution pass over 44 more directions (rays 20–63) is running now — not shown here yet.
