01 · The argument

You can measure a password. Can you measure a face?

We quantify how strong a password is with entropy — a number for its discriminatory power. Biometrics have no honest equivalent, and they carry faults a password never had. This project is an attempt to put a real number on a biometric authenticator, on the hardware it actually runs on.

Why this project exists

Real-time identity embeddings are already deployed. Live facial recognition at public events and on police vans, access control in workplaces, age and identity checks in retail, border e-gates, doorbells and dashcams. Most of it runs on small, power-constrained hardware: a quantised model on an edge SoC, often the same handful of published architectures behind a vendor’s own product name.

There is a real ethical argument about whether some of these deployments should exist at all. This project does not settle that argument and does not try to.

It addresses the question that sits underneath it. Suppose a use is one that society is willing to consent to. Consent is hard to give meaningfully when the thing being consented to has undefined behaviour — when neither the operator, the regulator, nor the person in front of the camera can say what the system will actually do at the edges. And an institution that wants to challenge a system, or defend its own use of one, currently has very little to challenge it with.

The evidence available today is aggregate: FAR and FRR at a chosen threshold, TAR at a fixed FAR on a benchmark such as IJB-C, demographic breakdowns of those same rates. All of it useful, all of it a population average. It is measured on a benchmark corpus, not on the identity or the population actually enrolled, and not on the device actually deployed. It reports how often the system is wrong, never in which direction — two systems with identical FAR can fail on completely different changes to a face. It says nothing about a specific enrolled person: “0.1% FAR” is no answer to “how much would someone have to look like me before this door opens”. And it is quoted for the model, while the thing installed is a quantised export of that model running on a specific chip — a different function, and nobody publishes the difference.

So a public-sector body procuring or contesting one of these systems can ask for the vendor’s numbers, and then has nowhere to go. The disagreement collapses to whose benchmark to believe.

What this project builds instead is a way to measure, for a specific enrolled identity on a specific deployed system, the shape of the region in embedding space the system still accepts as that person — the acceptance surface. That is evidence of a different kind: per-identity rather than per-population, directional rather than a single number (the first measured surface extends 7.6× further along some directions than others), measured on the deployed artefact rather than the paper model, and reproducible by a third party, so a claim can be disputed rather than taken on trust.

The intent is not to declare a system safe or unsafe. It is to make the behaviour legible enough that an informed decision is possible at all. See why the rig is built the way it is for the measurement apparatus and what each comparison isolates.

Who this is for

Not, principally, a penetration tester looking for a point-in-time bypass. The output of this work is not an exploit; it is a map.

The audience is the people who have to make or defend a decision about a deployment: the risk owner, the assurance function, the board-level oversight lead, the regulator, and the individual who has been enrolled. Embedding models behave as black-box continuous manifolds, and their failure modes — plateaus, cliffs, direction-dependent tolerance — are invisible in the evidence those people are normally handed. That leaves an institution unable to interrogate a system it is nonetheless accountable for: an ignorance that is structural rather than chosen, but an ignorance all the same, and one that persists precisely until somebody measures.

The intent is to replace “the vendor says the FAR is 0.1%” with something a non-specialist can be walked through, disagree with, and re-run. See per-identity discriminatory power for why two enrolled people may not get the same protection from the same system, and what this apparatus can ask about that.

What the work has shown so far

The claim that population rates hide per-identity behaviour is no longer an argument; it has been measured on this system. Every threshold between 0.27 and 0.60 gives zero false accepts and zero false rejects on the subject’s photographs and 1,164 strangers, and across that same band the region the system accepts as her goes from a tight star to one it barely bounds. An operator tuning on FAR and FRR sees a flat, perfect line while the thing underneath moves by forty degrees.

For one enrolled identity, measured to the end: the deployed INT8 pipeline still accepts altered versions of her 32–44° further from her than any real photograph sits (a median boundary of 67.4°, and 67.2° host / 67.4° device when re-judged against an enrolment template made on the pipeline’s own buffer — a correction found on 2026-09-18 that moved the template by 21° and the boundary by 0.2°); it is about 6–7° looser than the desktop-class model on the same images and twice as uneven by direction; and the chip reproduces the host to 0.4°. Twenty synthetic identities have now been measured the same way, host and device, and the effect the argument predicts is there: the same vendor threshold leaves some identities with strangers already inside their accepted region and others with none, and the looseness of the deployed model is not the same from person to person — the claim that it is looser than a desktop model turns out to be true of her and false of nine of the twenty. The twenty also put a limit on measuring without the device: across a cohort the host stands in for the chip, but for individuals it does not — five of the twenty-one differ by more than their measurement interval, and the identity the host called narrowest is not the one the chip does. An operator screening a cohort can work on the host; an operator asking which of their users is most exposed has to run the device.

Paths towards named strangers have now been walked (experiment 19), and on eight of ten synthetic pairs a single face exists that the pipeline accepts as either person, confirmed on the ESP32-S3 itself — the per-identity counterpart of an impersonation rate rather than a comparison of radii. What the work has still not done, in the order it matters for assurance: measure a real, consented population rather than synthetic stand-ins; and make the perturbation controllable rather than measured after the fact. The first two are in scope; the third is future work.

Why a generator at all

An operator does not get to see inside the vendor’s system. There is no access to the embedding it computes, no way to nudge that embedding and re-score it, often no documentation of the model beyond a threshold. The only interface is the one the system exposes in service: pixels in, a verdict and perhaps a score out. So the perturbation cannot be made where the system keeps its representation. It has to be made somewhere the operator controls.

That is the whole point of the generator. A person is carried into a space the operator owns — the generator’s latent, measured with a recogniser the operator chooses — perturbed there by known amounts in chosen directions, rendered back into pixels, and presented to the black box exactly as a camera would present them. The size of each perturbation is measured in the operator’s space, with the operator’s ruler; the verdict comes from the vendor’s. Nothing about the measurement depends on knowing how the vendor’s system works, only on being able to show it a face. Everything this project has had to solve — inversion, staying on the generator’s distribution, measuring the achieved angle after every render, the instrument’s own offsets — is the cost of that move, and the reason a better-behaved generator is the future work.

The operator cohort

The population that matters to an operator is not a benchmark and not a synthetic one: it is the people enrolled in their system. The honest way to measure it is for the operator to build that dataset themselves — several photographs of each consenting user, captured through the deployed scanner where practical or otherwise as ordinary photographs, enrolled by the vendor’s own procedure, with self-declared demographic information given for group reporting — and then to run the same measurement this project makes on it: invert each person’s anchor, perturb it with the generator by measured amounts in many directions, score every render with the deployed system, and find where acceptance stops. That evaluates the system for the current cohort, on the device actually installed, in the terms an operator needs: not only how often it fails, but for whom, how far, and in which directions.

Real users change what the synthetic arms could not. Anchors are centroids of several real photographs, as the subject’s is, rather than single synthetic images that fail inversion a third of the time. Within-person variation is real. Labels are the person’s own. And the impostors are the actual cohort, so “nearest stranger” becomes the enrolled colleague most likely to be accepted as you — the operator’s real threat model, which also makes the path from one user towards their nearest colleague the measurement to run: how far along it the system still accepts them is the per-identity counterpart of an impersonation rate.

From the photographs alone, before any generation, the operator also gets each user’s margin against everyone else enrolled and who their nearest neighbour is — a first pass that takes minutes and flags the thin cases — and, swept over the operating threshold, whether one threshold serves the whole cohort. All of it is re-runnable after every model update, firmware change or re-enrolment. What this requires under the GDPR — threats, controls and an example consent form — is on its own page; the protocol is written down in the project’s documentation.

Passwords have a yardstick

Entropy tells you how hard a secret is to guess — bits of unpredictability — and it makes concrete faults legible. Reuse: one leak opens many doors. Length: too few bits and it is brute-forced. You can compare two password policies with a number and argue about them honestly.

A face gets enrolled and thresholded, and then… what? “It works” is not a measurement. FAR / FRR are aggregates over a test set — they tell you an average, not what security your identity actually gets.

A face is a worse kind of secret

Even before the geometry, biometric embeddings carry structural faults that passwords do not:

You can’t rotate it

A leaked password is reset in seconds. You have one face. Once its embedding is enrolled or leaked, it is compromised for good — there is no reissue.

It may not stay in its lane

A passkey is scoped to one service. Two recognisers optimised for the same thing — identity — can share enough structure that a template, or an attack, crafted against one may map into another’s space. A biometric secret is not neatly bounded to where you gave it.

Its strength isn’t uniform

Two people at the same threshold do not necessarily get the same security — and that is exactly what the geometry below is about.

The edge bends the sphere

In an idealised model, the region a system still accepts as you is a clean ball around your identity — the same margin in every direction. If that held, one number would describe your security.

But an edge model runs under tight compute constraints and is INT8-quantised. Quantisation aliases the score surface: the accepted region is not a smooth sphere. It is anisotropic — wider in some directions than others — with plateaus and cliffs. So how far an impostor can drift and still be accepted depends on where in the space your identity sits.

Aggregate FAR / FRR numbers hide this completely, because they average over everyone. The person sitting in a wide, aliased pocket of the surface is easier to spoof than the average suggests — and no one measured it.

What this measures

Perturb an identity at known amplitudes and map exactly where a real device flips from accepting to rejecting it — in every direction. That surface is an honest picture of the authenticator’s discriminatory power for that identity, on that hardware: the biometric analogue of “how many bits.”

If a password’s strength is its entropy, a biometric’s strength is the shape of the region that still passes as you — and on the edge, that shape is not the same for everyone.

Why the ESP32-S3?

The hardware-in-the-loop rig runs on an Espressif ESP32-S3, and that choice has two reasons behind it.

The main one is practical: Espressif publish a genuine reference path end to end — the ESP-DL inference runtime, ESP-IDF, quantised face detection and recognition models, and documentation for all of it. That means the pipeline, sensor to embedding to accept/reject, can be stood up and inspected in full, rather than reverse-engineered from a sealed product. An assurance study needs exactly that.

The other is professional: this SoC turns up inside a lot of white-label products — door entry, access control — so findings on it transfer to devices already in the field, not just to a lab board.

One caveat: this is a target of convenience and representativeness, not a judgement on Espressif. The method — perturb an identity, map where a device flips from accepting to rejecting it — is meant to apply to any embedded recognition stack; see why the rig is built the way it is for how that generalises.
